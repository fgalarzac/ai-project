\section{The question and the track}

\textbf{Question.} When does a bank's loan rate, measured relative to peers in
the same product, maturity, currency, and month, predict lower rather than
higher future delinquency? This is a \textbf{Track B} project: it supplies the
missing agent problem behind my thesis on Peruvian commercial banks. The thesis
currently writes the predictive slope as a reduced-form difference between an
adverse-selection channel and a screening channel. The adaptation below makes
the bank choose screening, derives both channels from primitives, and states
the condition under which their ranking reverses with borrower opacity. The
object is a forecasting mechanism, not a causal effect of changing a posted
rate.

\section{The baseline and the model the thesis currently lacks}

The ingredients exist separately. In \citet{varian1980}, informed and
uninformed buyers generate price dispersion. In \citet{broecker1990}, banks
with imperfect credit tests compete in interest rates, and a rate cut can
improve the attracted pool. \citet{ruckes2004} endogenizes screening intensity
and obtains an inverse-U response to the prior probability of a good borrower.
\citet{dellariccia2006} shows how information asymmetries across banks can
switch an equilibrium between screening and pooling. My adaptation combines a
relative-price sorting margin with a continuous screening choice and makes
borrower opacity the moderator.

Let \(D\in\mathcal D=[\underline D,\overline D]\) be the bank's spread
relative to its peers and \(\kappa\in[0,1]\) the opacity of the borrower
segment. The game has two stages. First, the bank posts \(D\) and receives
continuous loan demand \(L(D,\kappa)>0\), with \(L_D<0\). Second, after observing the
applicant pool, it chooses screening effort \(e\in[0,\bar e]\). Before
screening, the probability of default among potential originations is
\begin{equation}
q^0(D,\kappa)=\bar q+a(\kappa)D, \qquad a(\kappa)>0.
\label{eq:pool}
\end{equation}
The coefficient \(a\) is the sorting effect of a relative price: a higher
spread loses comparison shoppers and worsens the remaining pool. Screening
lowers default according to
\begin{equation}
q(D,e,\kappa)=q^0(D,\kappa)-g(\kappa)e,
\label{eq:quality}
\end{equation}
and costs \(c(\kappa)e^2/2\) per loan. A performing loan pays
\(R(D)=\bar R+D>0\), while \(F\) collects funding and operating costs that do
not depend on screening. The bank's problem is
\begin{equation}
\max_{D\in\mathcal D}\;L(D,\kappa)
\left\{\max_{e\in[0,\bar e]}
 [1-q^0(D,\kappa)+g(\kappa)e]R(D)-F-
 \frac{c(\kappa)}2e^2\right\}.
\label{eq:problem}
\end{equation}
Compactness and continuity give a first-stage solution. The proposed result is
about the continuation quality map \(q^*(D,\kappa)\), which is defined for any
observed relative price, including the equilibrium price and price variation
generated by bank-specific cost or markup shocks. This keeps the forecasting
claim separate from a causal comparative static of \(D^*\).

\section{The result I expect}

For an interior screening choice, the first- and second-order conditions are
\begin{equation}
g(\kappa)R(D)-c(\kappa)e=0,
\qquad \frac{\partial^2\pi}{\partial e^2}=-c(\kappa)<0.
\label{eq:foc}
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}
e^*(D,\kappa)=\frac{g(\kappa)}{c(\kappa)}(\bar R+D),
\quad
q^*(D,\kappa)=\bar q+a(\kappa)D-h(\kappa)(\bar R+D),
\quad
h(\kappa)\equiv\frac{g(\kappa)^2}{c(\kappa)}.
\label{eq:solution}
\end{equation}
The new object \(h\) is \emph{screening leverage}: the effect on portfolio
quality of the screening response induced by a larger loan payoff.

\textbf{Candidate Proposition 1 (opacity threshold).} Suppose: (i) the
solution is interior and \(q^*\in(0,1)\); (ii) \(a\) and \(h\) are continuous;
(iii) \(a'(\kappa)<0\); (iv) \(h'(\kappa)>0\), equivalently
\(2g'(\kappa)/g(\kappa)>c'(\kappa)/c(\kappa)\); and (v)
\(a(0)>h(0)\) while \(a(1)<h(1)\). Then there is a unique
\(\kappa^*\in(0,1)\) satisfying \(a(\kappa^*)=h(\kappa^*)\), and
\begin{equation}
\beta(\kappa)\equiv\frac{\partial q^*}{\partial D}
=a(\kappa)-h(\kappa)
\begin{cases}
>0,&\kappa<\kappa^*,\\
=0,&\kappa=\kappa^*,\\
<0,&\kappa>\kappa^*.
\end{cases}
\qquad
\frac{\partial^2q^*}{\partial D\partial\kappa}=a'-h'<0.
\label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
The proof is the intermediate value theorem plus strict monotonicity. If future
delinquency satisfies \(m_{t+s}=\rho_s q_t^*+\varepsilon_{t+s}\), with
\(\rho_s>0\), its predictive slope has the same sign. Importantly, higher
screening cost alone makes \(h\) smaller and cannot deliver the reversal. The
economic restriction is that opacity raises the marginal information gain
enough to dominate any increase in cost. This is a falsifiable sufficient
condition, not a definition disguised as a result.

\section{Why this is not already solved}

I checked the model and appendices of the accessible 2004 version of
\citet{ruckes2004}, the 2005 working-paper record and the full IMF reprint of
\citet{dellariccia2006}, and the stated equilibrium result of
\citet{broecker1990}. Broecker fixes the testing technology and studies how
rate competition sorts borrowers. Ruckes solves for screening intensity and
mixed loan pricing as economic prospects change, but does not characterize the
sign of relative price as a default signal across opacity segments.
Dell'Ariccia and Marquez vary information asymmetry across banks and derive
screening-versus-pooling regimes, not a continuous price-to-quality derivative.
Varian supplies the shopper/captive-buyer logic but has neither screening nor
default. The proposed derivative \(a(\kappa)-h(\kappa)\), its threshold, and
the distinction between costly screening and valuable screening therefore do
not appear in those baselines.

\section{Plan and risks}

I will prove Proposition 1 and a robustness version with a general increasing
repayment function \(R(D)\), simulate the full parameter region rather than a
single calibration, and formalize the threshold theorem in Lean after freezing
the paper proposition. The code already checks the FOC, SOC, crossing,
monotonicity, interiority, and probability bounds for a transparent
calibration. The empirical thesis will map the theoretical \(\kappa\) to
pre-specified segment-level opacity proxies and test out-of-sample forecasts;
it will not label the estimated coefficient causal.

The main risk is condition (iv). If the data or a richer signal technology do
not support increasing screening leverage, I will not force a sign reversal.
The fallback result will characterize three regions, \(a>h\), \(a=h\), and
\(a<h\), and treat the existence of a threshold as an estimable restriction.
A second risk is an endogenous-price confound; the fallback is to keep the
theorem explicitly conditional on observed \(D\), add bank and
segment-by-month controls, and interpret the exercise only as forecasting.
